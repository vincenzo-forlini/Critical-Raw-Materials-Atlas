About this map

Where the European Union's critical raw materials are mined, processed, refined, smelted, recycled and recovered — and who operates each site.

This is a seed dataset. It was compiled from public knowledge as a starting point, and no row has been verified against its source. Ownership, capacity and operating status change often in this industry. Every site carries a confidence flag and a source link — check them before citing anything here.

What it covers

The 34 critical raw materials listed in the EU Critical Raw Materials Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252), with the 17 strategic raw materials flagged. Sites are recorded across the EU and the wider European neighbourhood — Norway, the United Kingdom, Iceland, the Western Balkans, Ukraine and Turkey — because EU supply chains depend on them.

Each site is classified by its stage in the chain:

StageMeaning
MiningExtraction of ore or mineral from the ground
ProcessingCrushing, grinding and concentration of ore
RefiningChemical separation into a purified product
SmeltingThermal reduction of concentrate into metal
RecyclingBulk scrap reprocessed back into the same material
RecoveryA minor critical raw material extracted from a complex waste stream
SubstitutionReplacing a critical raw material with something less constrained

Recycling and recovery are deliberately separate. Recycling turns copper scrap back into copper cathode: the material you want is the bulk of what goes in. Recovery pulls out something that is a small fraction of a complicated mixture — neodymium from a magnet, lithium from black mass, silicon from a solar panel, germanium from a zinc refining residue. The second is a much harder problem, and in Europe it is where most of the newer companies are working.

On the map, the pictogram and the colour both encode the stage. Encoding it twice is deliberate: it keeps the map legible when markers are small, and readable when hue alone is not available. The colours were chosen by maximising the minimum perceptual distance across normal, protanopic, deuteranopic and tritanopic vision — an earlier palette looked perfectly good and had made smelting and recycling identical to a deuteranopic reader. Materials are not a marker channel: narrow the material filter and the whole map becomes that material's map.

EU Strategic Projects

Sites forming part of a Strategic Project designated under the Critical Raw Materials Act carry a ★ Project label and can be filtered on their own. These are the most authoritative rows in the dataset: project name, promoter, country, materials and value-chain stage are transcribed from the Commission's annex to decision C(2025) 1904 of 25 March 2025, rather than compiled.

The promoting company stays the headline. A card reads Eramet with a Project label, not Ageli — who is building the thing matters more than the project's codename.

The Act uses four value-chain categories — extraction, processing, recycling and substitution — which are coarser than the seven stages used here. Both are recorded, so neither has to be guessed from the other.

46 of the 47 designated EU projects are on the map. Seventeen of them are sites that were already in the dataset and were flagged rather than duplicated. The single absence is the NGC Battery Materials graphite initiative: its plant has not been built and no site has been announced, so there is nowhere honest to put a marker. Several projects that are shown had their town inferred rather than published; those rows say so and carry lower confidence. The 13 non-EU projects designated in June 2025 are not yet loaded.

The periodic table view

Explore the CRMs shows the periodic table with every listed material highlighted — gold for strategic, blue for the rest — and the number of European sites in the corner of each cell. Clicking an element opens its factsheet.

The EU list does not map one-to-one onto the table, and the view shows that rather than hiding it. Platinum group metals and the two rare earth groups are highlighted across every member, each opening the same group factsheet. Baryte, fluorspar, phosphate rock and feldspar are minerals, not elements, so they sit on a separate row beneath the table. Carbon is both natural graphite and coking coal — two separate entries on the EU list, one element — so it opens both factsheets in turn.

The Act designates 17 strategic raw materials; this dataset shows 18 strategic rows, because the Act treats rare earths for magnets as one entry while the dataset keeps light and heavy rare earths apart. The view says so where the count appears.

Precision

Locations are recorded at city level only. The dataset says that a mine is at Kiruna and a smelter is at Hamburg; it does not record street addresses or pit coordinates. Every site in one city at one stage shares a single marker, with a count badge when there is more than one.

This is a deliberate limit rather than missing detail. Publishing a precise coordinate the compiler never checked would imply an accuracy the dataset does not have.

How the data is organised

Everything is plain CSV in data/, meant to be opened in a spreadsheet and corrected by hand. There is no build step: edit a file, save it, refresh the page.

FileWhat it holds
facilities.csvOne row per site, with a tick column per material
cities.csvCoordinates, held in exactly one place
companies.csvOperator details, website, news feed, company type, logo
elements.csvThe material encyclopedia and EU indicators
production.csvWho produces how much, by year — drives the charts
news.jsonGenerated by CI, not hand-edited

Adding a site

  1. Add a row to facilities.csv and put an x in each material column that applies.
  2. If its city is new, add a row to cities.csv with latitude and longitude.
  3. If its operator is new, add a row to companies.csv.
  4. Run node scripts/validate-data.mjs to check it holds together.

Conventions

  • A tick column accepts x, 1, true, yes or sì. Blank means no.
  • Multi-value fields are separated by |, and label/value pairs by :: — for example China::68|Chile::24. The pipe is used rather than a semicolon because a semicolon may be the CSV delimiter itself.
  • Both comma and semicolon delimiters are read automatically, so saving from a European Excel install will not break the map. Files carry a UTF-8 byte order mark so that names like Rönnskär survive the round trip.

Company type

Companies carry a maturity of incumbent, scale-up or startup. "Incumbent" is the standard term for the opposite of a startup, and it is the default — a blank cell means an ordinary established operator, not missing information.

Spin-offs are recorded as start-ups. Almost every university spin-out in this sector is also a start-up, and separating the two labels would split the filter without telling you anything you could act on. Where a company is a spin-off, it says so in the company's description.

Company logos

The expanded company panel shows the operator's logo, taken from that company's own website and stored in the repository rather than hot-linked — so the map still works offline, and no company's server learns who is browsing this dataset. Logos are trademarks of their owners and appear only to identify the company whose sites are shown.

Where no logo could be fetched, a coloured monogram of the company's initials is drawn instead. That is the intended resting state rather than a failure, and no other company's mark is ever substituted to fill the gap.

Operators in difficulty

Companies carry a status: active by default, or insolvency, liquidation, acquired or dissolved, with a note giving the detail and date. Where it is set, a warning chip appears next to the operator wherever it is named.

This is recorded against the company rather than the site, because the two really can differ: a plant may run normally while its owner is in court. Larco's Larymna works and Northvolt's recycling arm are both cases where the site status and the company status say different things, and only keeping both preserves that.

The flag exists because of a genuine conflict in the sources. The CRM Act annex of March 2025 lists NorthCYCLE as an ongoing Strategic Project; Northvolt filed for bankruptcy in Sweden the same month. Neither source is wrong. The map shows both rather than quietly choosing one.

Confidence flags

Every site carries one, and the app displays it rather than hiding weak rows.

  • High — a long-established, well-documented fact, such as which company operates a major mine.
  • Medium — correct in outline, but ownership or status has changed recently enough to be worth re-checking.
  • Low — included because the site matters, but a detail is genuinely uncertain. Treat as a lead, not a fact.

last_checked is blank on every seeded row, because none has been verified. Fill it in as you check them.

Figures in the encyclopedia

Supply shares, import reliance, recycling rates and substitution indices are indicative. They are the kind of figures published in the EU's raw materials assessments, reproduced here to give a sense of scale, not to serve as a citable source. The linked references on each material are where the real numbers live.

The share chart shows a "rest of world" slice wherever the listed producers do not add up to 100%. Shares are never rescaled to fill the circle, because that would quietly promote a 52% producer to 58%.

production.csv holds one row per material, stage, country and year, so adding more years turns the single-year snapshot into a trend line automatically. Good public sources for those numbers are the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries, the World Mining Data yearbook, and Eurostat.

Company news

The news shown on a company is taken from that company's own newsroom feed, set in the news_feed column of companies.csv. A scheduled GitHub Action fetches them daily and commits the result; the page then reads a local file.

It works this way for three reasons: an API key committed to a public static site is public, browsers block cross-origin feed requests, and news aggregator feeds generally licence their output for personal feed readers only. A company's own press-release feed is published in order to be syndicated. Only the headline, publisher, date and link are stored — never article text.

Corrections

Corrections are the point of the CSV format. If you know a row is wrong, edit it, set its confidence and last_checked, and the map updates on refresh.
